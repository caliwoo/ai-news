## Top stories today

### Goodfire: AI's "black box" is more readable than we thought

Interpretability lab Goodfire released a paper, "Neurons as an Interpretable Basis," showing that by examining and prodding a model's individual neurons you can interpret what they do, predict which prompts make them fire, and influence their behavior. The work was tested on a 35-billion-parameter Qwen model (Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, about 5 million neurons). Co-founder and chief scientist Tom McGrath says research agents did much of the work, so work that once took very intelligent people a month of prodding is now a month of agent time they have on tap. He is optimistic it scales, since sparser, better models seem more interpretable, but warns the cost could reach about $100 million for a trillion-parameter model at roughly $1 per neuron. Why it matters: he sees interpretability as a path to a real lie detector for models, to controlling what gets learned in training, and to mitigating reward hacking. He also says alignment is falling behind model capability.

## Building with AI

### From prompt engineering to context engineering

Dharmesh Shah argues that with context windows growing from 4K to over 1M tokens, the key skill is now deciding what the model has access to, not just how you ask. He walks through how chat memory, RAG and tool calling all work by filling the context window. He describes the context engineer's job in four verbs: curate, structure, compress and evaluate, watching for "context dilution." One caveat: cost and latency grow roughly linearly with window length.

*Sources: Simple AI*

## Science & tech (Sunday special)

### SpaceX catches Starship after a full orbital mission

After a 22-hour orbital flight, Starship flew back to Texas and was caught by the launch tower's mechanical arms, the first time this has been done with Starship. The booster was not caught. It's a step toward a fully reusable rocket.

*Sources: Superhuman AI*

### AI helps steer a brain implant for a blind man

Scientists trained AI to predict how a blind man's visual cortex would respond to signals from a 96-electrode implant. It picked stimulation patterns that produced the intended activity more accurately. Sight isn't restored yet, but this could lead to more reliable artificial vision.

*Sources: Superhuman AI*
