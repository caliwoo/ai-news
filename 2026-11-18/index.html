Models & Launches

OpenAI ships GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol to harsh early reviews

OpenAI released GPT-6.2 Astra (frontier model for the hardest projects) and GPT-6.2 Sol (efficient everyday model), rolling out to Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and Codex and to developers via the API; both use fewer output tokens per result. On Artificial Analysis’s ranking, Astra now sits ahead of Google’s Gemini 4 Carbon and behind only Anthropic’s Fable 5.2 (a 5-point climb since September), while Sol matches September’s GPT-6 Astra at roughly 15% of the cost per task. Astra leads tests in science, legal work, robotics and 3D builds but shows no meaningful coding gain, and Sol regresses from GPT-6.1 in coding. OpenAI rated Astra at its “High” threshold for AI self-improvement, a first for a publicly released model. Several testers panned the release, one calling it “the worst model OpenAI has ever released.” Why it matters: the launch lands days before rival Anthropic’s IPO. Opinion (The Rundown): anything short of passing the latest Claude models was likely to be a letdown, and OpenAI’s next answer “can’t be another No. 2.”

Update: Jensen Huang apologizes and says he’d return as Nvidia CEO

In his first public statement since resigning last week, Huang apologized for “serious mistakes,” said he “created problems for the Board” and will cooperate with its probe, and said he would “be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO” if it is in the best interest of shareholders, employees, the company and America. He did not mention China, Alice Huang or the reported affairs. The petition for his return has passed 188,000 signatures, and Nvidia employee Nader Khalil warned the board that “irreplaceable talent” is considering leaving. Board chair Harvey Jones said the company “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO” (CNBC reported the board is focused on the national-security investigation), though Semafor reports some directors are still pushing for his return and Huang has spoken with at least one board member. Acting CEO Colette Kress, speaking on Tuesday’s earnings call, said “our momentum is real.”

IMF warns AI circular financing has passed $1 trillion

IMF managing director Kristalina Georgieva told CNBC that circular financing (investors funding companies that hand the money back to buy their products) has grown to just over $1 trillion, two-thirds of it debt-funded: “We have a world economy that is standing on one leg on the AI leg.” Nvidia’s equity investments reached $99 billion as of July, up from about $7 billion a year earlier, with nearly $50 billion in frontier labs. The WSJ reports interim CEO Colette Kress was skeptical of many deals and Huang had begun planning to scale them back; one analyst note predicts a pullback could take $50 billion or more out of the market. Columbia professor Shiva Rajgopal told The Deep View that smaller AI clouds and model developers would feel it first. Opinion (The Deep View): the AI technology itself “is not going anywhere,” whatever happens to Nvidia’s leadership.

Links: CNBC · WSJ

Safety & Policy

OpenAI embeds four independent auditors inside the company

OpenAI signed agreements with Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire as the first phase of “embedded assessments,” giving them the same internal access as its researchers: model checkpoints, source code, data, monitoring systems and documentation. Apollo will test whether models can covertly collude against users; Goodfire will monitor and debug training runs; METR will look for misalignment in OpenAI’s “largest reinforcement learning run to date” and run a red-team stress test; Transluce will evaluate post-training alignment of public and unreleased models. OpenAI will publish some findings but keeps the right to redact confidential information and request disclosure delays. Anthropic made a similar move with METR in October. Opinion (The Deep View / AI for Work): a welcome step, but the real test is how much the public gets to see.

Cybersecurity

“Java4Shell”: a Java flaw lets attackers take over Jira, Jenkins and Keycloak without a login

Oracle and OpenJDK patched CVE-2026-114938, a memory bug in Java’s Base64 decoding found by research firm Calif, which says GPT-6 Cyber helped it find the flaw. Calif’s attacks worked on default installs of Keycloak, Jira, Confluence, JFrog Artifactory, Jenkins and PingFederate, and it says it broke into a login system used by NASA and CERN. It affects Java 17.0.6 and later (including 21, 25 and 27) only on Intel and AMD chips with AVX-512 VBMI. Oracle scores it 8.1; Red Hat’s preliminary score is 9.8. Other labs reproduced crashes and a memory leak but not code execution, and Canada’s Cyber Centre knows of no attacks. What to do: update to patched Oracle, Amazon Corretto or Azul Zulu builds (Red Hat has no fix yet), restart Java services and rebuild containers; if you can’t patch, launch Java with -XX:-UseBASE64Intrinsics. Separately, OpenAI said NATO’s tech and cyber agency is using its Daybreak security models (up to 300B tokens a month) and that ENISA used its models to find four flaws in EU institutions’ software, all now fixed.

Links: CVE · Calif write-up · The Rundown: Calif · NATO + OpenAI

Google: hackers now use AI agents to turn patches into exploits automatically

Google’s Threat Intelligence Group found an attack framework that compares code before and after a security update, works out which flaw was fixed, and builds and launches an exploit with no human involved. Google says it is the first case it has seen, declared “the era of autonomous attacks has arrived,” and expects basic attacks like this to spread within one to three months. Why it matters: the gap between a patch’s release and your rollout is now the window to close.

Links: Google report · The Rundown

Update: outage week adds Chase, and the Microsoft and FAA causes get clearer

A software bug in an on-premises hardware system at JPMorgan Chase disrupted banking for hours Monday, delaying payments at other banks whenever Chase was involved (Downdetector logged nearly 5,000 Chase complaints); Chase found no sign of a cyberattack, and it was its biggest failure since 2010. United Airlines also fixed a Monday outage that delayed departures. New on earlier outages (covered yesterday): Microsoft says its global outage came from a months-old, human-written Windows change (not AI-written code), faulty network connections and reduced server capacity; and The Deep View notes AI suspicion around the FAA ground stop arose because the FAA’s $875 million AI system SMART, which merges 200 data streams, went nationwide on November 10, three days earlier; House members had already requested an audit of it. The Director of National Intelligence dismissed malicious causes, while researchers say AI-accelerated bug discovery is forcing record patching that is itself disrupting services. One knock-on: a German theme park blames last week’s Outlook outage for an alert that never arrived, reportedly injuring 15 pool guests.

Big data centers rival Denmark’s emissions

Harvard researchers analyzing 403 U.S. hyperscale data centers estimate they used 82 TWh between May 2024 and April 2025 (about 1.8% of U.S. demand) and emitted about 25.7 million metric tons of CO2, comparable to Denmark. About 54% of their power came from fossil fuels; Virginia, Oregon, Ohio and Iowa accounted for more than half the use.

How teams keep coding agents in check

The Claude Code team treats verification as running the changed system and collecting evidence, with end-to-end, property and differential tests, which is what lets teams run hundreds of agents in parallel. Anthropic says a “chaos loop” helped Claude Docs create 4.6 million docs in six days with only 60 server errors across 2 billion requests.
