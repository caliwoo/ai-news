Research & Science

Anthropic mathematician says he and Claude proved the Riemann hypothesis

Anthropic’s Levent Alpöge says he used Claude to prove the Riemann hypothesis, the 167-year-old question about how prime numbers are distributed, after about six weeks of work. First posed in 1859, it carries a $1M Clay Millennium Prize. Alpöge shared the proof only as 17M lines of machine-checkable math (he wrote that Claude “flew, and danced, and sparkled near that finish line”), and the groups behind the Lean proof language certified it on Saturday, saying they have “full confidence” it is correct, even though no human fully understands it yet. Not everyone is thrilled: the Association for Human Mathematics called it “little more than publicity” for AI labs, and a rival, reportedly AI-free proof from Alessandro Masullo (nearly 12 years of work) surfaced hours later, though others quickly flagged problems in it. Why it matters: top mathematicians expected this to take decades of new theory. Opinion (TLDR Dev): a post argues the result is a sign superintelligence is approaching, and the industry should build AI that keeps people in control.

Links: The Rundown · Lean certification · Criticism · Rival proof · TLDR summary · “Superintelligence Is Arriving”

OpenAI publishes its internal ‘intelligence’ scorecard and asks rivals to agree on limits

OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published the internal metric OpenAI has used since GPT-3.5 to track raw model capability: log-perplexity on a held-out dataset (how well a model predicts the next token; lower is better). Scores fell from about 0.99 for GPT-3.5 (the model behind the original ChatGPT) to 0.91 for GPT-4; OpenAI ties its biggest new cybersecurity and math skills to scores below 0.8, revealed an unreleased internal model at about 0.73, and says to “expect further transformative capabilities” around 0.68–0.70. Pachocki proposes using perplexity alongside other measures to set shared safety thresholds that labs would hold until monitoring tools catch up, while acknowledging different datasets make cross-lab comparisons hard. Sam Altman called it “the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while.” Why it matters: it is a renewed call for industry-wide limits plus a first look at where OpenAI’s next model stands; The Rundown notes that, like METR’s report on Anthropic, it suggests acceleration is not slowing.

Links: OpenAI post · Altman · METR report · TLDR summary

Also in research: Wikipedia compression, Neuralink’s trials and AI in biology

A researcher’s “tufazip” compressed one billion bytes of Wikipedia into 105,388,353 bytes, weights and decoder included, using a 12-layer, 163M-parameter Transformer initialized from a 34M-parameter offline model quantized to 4.33 MB. A long Life feature reports Neuralink has sped up surgeries and extended trials to Canada and the UK; early participants coach new ones, but trial terms include no upgrades, so without a commercial device they will eventually return to life without the implant. Opinion: an essay argues AI in biology is “not just a data problem”: what is measured and its quality matter, and there is still no clear path to a full account of how a cell or tissue works.

Links: tufazip · Neuralink (Life) · AI x Bio

Thinking Machines releases Inkling 2 Mini, an open-weights coding model for a 32GB Mac

Mira Murati’s Thinking Machines Lab released Inkling 2 Mini, a small open-weights model aimed at coding agents. It runs its middle layers three times per token, uses about 20 GB of memory (it can run on a 32GB Mac), and the lab reports 48.2% on DeepSWE v1.1, noting evaluation setups vary across models. Why it matters: competitive agentic-coding performance on a laptop lowers the bar for local, private coding agents; TLDR Dev also links a beginner’s guide to running quantized Inkling or Qwen models locally with Unsloth Desktop on Windows.

Cybersecurity

One malicious webpage was enough to hijack Meta’s Muse agent

Security firm NebuSec used a single malicious webpage to pull a user’s private chats and saved memories from Meta Muse, the personal agent that acts through a user’s connected accounts; the chats and memories could also be altered, and Muse’s network-approval prompt never fired as data left. The chain: a flaw in Muse’s bundled Chromium browser gave code execution in the agent’s container, then a Linux kernel flaw gave root on the user’s cloud VM, exposing stored credentials, files and Sentinel, the monitoring layer Muse’s security design relies on to gate outbound data. It stayed confined to one user’s machine, and every exploit was patched before publication on November 20. Why it matters: it follows reports (covered Nov 20) that Muse mixed up chats and exposed data. The Code’s advice: tightly scope agent credentials on the assumption a sandbox escape is possible, and review the whole stack an agent runs on, browser and OS included, against chained attacks.

Links: NebuSec write-up · Technical breakdown · Muse security design

Prompt theft at AI hosts, a leaky AI supply chain and patches to apply

OpenRouter dropped three providers after Dragos found attackers had been stealing prompts from AI hosting firms for months. Researchers who planted 2M canaries in the AI supply chain found an ecosystem leaking highly sensitive user data (Ars Technica). CISA says attackers are exploiting flaws in VMware vCenter, Cisco Expressway, SAP and Apache Roller; a malicious key can corrupt memory in apps using the GPGME library (CVE-2026-129975, fixed in 2.3.1); and WhatsApp fixed three flaws, one of which (CVE-2026-23871) it suspects spammers abused to expose Android users’ IP information. Trending: a new FBI alert says AI agents can break into a company in under 3 hours, and 1Password shows how they did it without a single new exploit; a phishing scam uses a fake Lego partnership and a fake Calendly page to steal email logins. Resources: Anthropic’s guide to securing enterprise agents (prompt injection, memory poisoning, sandboxing, scoped access) and a Claude Code vulnerability-scanner skill.

Coding agents in practice: 100+ PRs a week, a vibe-coded chip and stale memory

A post describes shipping 100+ PRs to production in a week; many are simple, but the author sees it as a sign software fundamentals have changed. A developer vibe-coded an FPGA into running an LLM, showing what a large token budget can now reach. Other reads: persistent agent memory can feed stale facts back after circumstances change, so background passes that review and rewrite memories are needed; “informal verification” uses fast models as proof checkers alongside formal methods; teams can make agents more reliable by turning recurring workflows into reusable patterns with self-checks. New tools: It’s a Plan, a self-hosted issue tracker where AI agents get roles, permissions and assignments alongside humans, and Project Chaperone, an open-source harness that enforces data-flow, action, output and network rules on agents at runtime. Lesson learned: Nango, after a bug discarded rotated OAuth refresh tokens, now saves replacement tokens immediately and allows only one refresh per connection at a time.
